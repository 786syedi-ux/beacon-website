Why 2026 succession plans need a bench, not a chart

August 2026·Beacon

In the first half of 2025 alone, a record 1,235 CEOs left their roles — and a third of their replacements were interim leaders, roughly triple the rate seen the year before. That's not a story about CEOs. It's a story about how few of those companies had a genuinely ready successor when the moment came.

Most organisations we talk to have a succession chart — a slide reviewed once a year, naming a person or two next to each critical role. Far fewer have a succession bench: candidates who've been assessed against the actual demands of the role, given a real development plan, and tested in stretch assignments before the seat is empty. The difference between the two only shows up at the worst possible moment.

It's getting harder to get away with the chart version. The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025 found that employers expect 39% of the core skills required in today's jobs to change by 2030. A successor named against last year's job description may not be the right successor for the role as it exists eighteen months from now — which means succession planning has quietly become a continuous exercise, not an annual one.

What a real bench looks like in practice: two to three assessed candidates per critical role, not one; a named readiness horizon for each (ready now, ready in 1–2 years, ready in 3+); a specific development action attached to each — not just a label; and a refresh cadence of twice a year, not once, so the plan tracks the role instead of the org chart.

If your last succession review was a single meeting before the board pack was due, that's usually the first place we'd look.
