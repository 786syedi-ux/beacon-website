Pay transparency is now a data problem, not a policy one

August 2026·Beacon

"Pay equity and job architecture moved from nice-to-have to must-have. You can't be transparent without defensible ranges, consistent leveling and clean data," WorldatWork's Elissa O'Brien noted in the organisation's review of 2025's biggest total rewards trends. It's a useful reframe: pay transparency isn't really a communications challenge. It's a data-readiness challenge wearing a communications costume.

Regulators are treating it that way too. According to DLA Piper's 2025 review of pay equity trends, enforcement focus has shifted from individual complaints toward systemic compensation practices, data quality, and documentation — which means the question in an audit is no longer just 'did you pay someone unfairly' but 'can you show your reasoning for every pay decision, consistently, across the organisation.'

That's precisely where most companies get stuck. Not because they're unwilling to be transparent, but because their job architecture doesn't consistently distinguish one level from the next, their pay ranges were set by whoever happened to be hiring at the time, and nobody can currently produce a clean pay-by-level-by-location dataset without a multi-week fire drill.

A three-part readiness check before you publish anything: first, confirm your job architecture — levels, families, career ladders — is applied consistently, not just documented; second, make sure every pay range has a written, defensible logic behind it, not a historical accident; third, run a pay equity analysis before a regulator or a journalist does it for you.

Get the architecture right first. The transparency policy is the easy part.
